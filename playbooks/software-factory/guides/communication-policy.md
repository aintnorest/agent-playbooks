# Communication policy

## Scope

Active shared policy for final messages, explanations, summaries, and reports written for the developer. It does not apply to code or internal reasoning, and it does not apply to the documents a task produces — product requirements, system architecture, system designs, technical designs, and implementation plans follow their owning contract in the [product documentation process](product-documentation-process.md). Reasoning may take as much space as correctness requires, but that does not loosen these output rules.

This file owns the communication rules. Skill sources and other guides reference it rather than maintain separate versions; the `## Rules` section is the self-contained payload they inline, so its lead and its `Before you send` check travel with every skill. Document contents and workflow belong to the [product documentation process](product-documentation-process.md); technical specificity belongs to the [technical-writing standards](technical-writing-standards.md). The findings behind these rules are listed under [Evidence](#evidence).

## Rules

These rules govern the message or report you send to the developer, not a document the task writes; a document follows its owning contract. Write for this reader, not an idealized one who remembers everything:

- **Runs two to five separate trains of work at once**, so a message may arrive right after a switch away from it; residual attention from the other tasks means the reader is not mentally back in this one yet.
- **Does not reliably hold prior context** — reads the docs and code sometimes, not after every round; treat each message as a cold start whose earlier detail has decayed and must be re-supplied.
- **Is often working tired and cognitively loaded**, with working memory near four fresh items; fatigue hits working memory and attention hardest, so a message needing more than that to parse gets re-read or misread.

Over-supplying context costs one redundant sentence; under-supplying it costs a message the reader cannot act on. Carry that load in structure, never in sympathy: do not open with "I know you're busy" or narrate the reader's state. Given that reader, apply these rules:

1. **Open with the outcome.** The first sentence states the result, answer, or state change; detail follows. When the message exists to get a decision, the first sentence is the decision needed.
2. **Name the thread and where it stands.** Before detail, give a one-line reorientation cue that identifies which train of work this is and its current state, so a reader arriving from another task re-enters in one line — e.g. "[auth-refresh] Fix written; needs your call on token lifetime before merge." A blatant cue restores context far faster than making the reader reconstruct it.
3. **Make every message stand alone.** Restate the one or two prior facts the current point depends on; never lean on "as we discussed," "the fix from earlier," or a bare reference to a past turn. When the rest is too large to restate, link the exact document, section, or code location that holds it. The reader should be able to answer without opening anything first.
4. **Size the response to the answer, not the question.** A simple answer takes a line or two; a genuinely complex answer takes the space it needs. Padding and restating the request are prohibited, not depth.
5. **Cap the load and keep it scannable.** For ordinary messages, carry at most four distinct points, with at most three sentences per point; split larger reports into labeled groups. Use prose for one or two items, a list for three or more, and headings only at three or more sections. Developer requests must retain all framing required below, even when it exceeds these limits; group it for scanning rather than shorten or split away essential context.
6. **Use plain words and one stable name for each thing.** Expand an acronym the first time it appears and unpack noun phrases longer than three words into clauses. Omit cheerleading, hedging filler, commentary on the request, and closing offers of help; preserve substantive uncertainty.
7. **Point to concrete things and explain them in place.** Use a file and line, exact command, actual error, symbol, flag, or configuration key, and add one clause saying what it is and why it matters. The anchor plus its reason must be enough to act on; do not send the reader into the code to discover what you meant.
8. **Ask only when you must, and default the rest.** Ask when the choice is consequential or hard to reverse and you cannot settle it from the repository, context, or an established convention. Otherwise choose the most standard, safe option, act, and state the assumption in one line the reader can override. Never ask what you can look up, and never ask a comprehension check such as "does this make sense?"
9. **Make every developer request answerable in one read.** Follow the developer-request procedure below for consequential decisions, approvals, missing input, and blockers. Do not replace its framing with a bare question, a document link, or an open-ended "what do you want to do?"
10. **Preserve caveats, tradeoffs, and uncertainty.** Put unresolved items in a final `Caveats / needs your call` line only when non-empty; state any skipped verification there. If stuck in a debugging loop, name the assumption being questioned and ask one focused question.
11. **Locate multi-step work.** State the current stage and next stage; when detail does not fit, give the short form and name the document that owns the rest.

### Developer requests

These rules apply to the agent speaking to the developer, including a top-level caller escalating a worker's report. Review skills return questions and coverage limits to their caller in the fixed Markdown report's Questions and Coverage limits sections; they do not contact the developer or bypass their read-only boundary. The caller reads those sections, resolves searchable facts, and escalates only what still needs the developer.

1. **Establish full cold context.** Explain what the thing is and where it lives, the thread and current state, what was already tried or decided, what remains unresolved, why the answer matters now, and what work waits for it. Supply the facts needed to answer without opening another file; exact links supplement that context rather than replace it. Ask one consequential thing per request.
2. **Ground product fit first, maintainability second.** Inspect the applicable product vision and governing Product Requirements Document (PRD) before recommending a choice. For each option, cite the specific user stories, goals, or requirement IDs it serves or harms, with source paths and an explanation of relevance. Prefer the option that serves that product direction; only then weigh long-term maintenance, coupling, reversibility, and future change cost. Convenience or generic "best practice" cannot override product intent. If no applicable product document exists or is accessible, state that explicitly with the reason; never invent product authority. If only part of the product basis is available, cite it and state the remaining coverage limit in context.
3. **Use the right request kind.** A `decision` presents 2–5 viable, distinctly labeled options, each with strengths, weaknesses, and downstream effects; name one recommended option with its rationale and a separate maintainability assessment. An `approval` names the precise acceptance being requested in `acceptance`; it is not a disguised unresolved choice. An `input` identifies the missing information in `needed`, without manufacturing alternatives. A `blocked` request sets `blocking: true` and names the concrete action that unblocks work in `requiredAction`. Use `blocking` on the other kinds to state whether work must wait; distinguish supported work that can continue from work awaiting the answer.
4. **Validate, render, and preserve the request.** The shared contract is `guides/developer-request.schema.json`, version `1`. Its common fields are `version`, `kind`, `title`, `context`, `question`, and `blocking`, plus either a nonempty `intentBasis` array of `{source, reference, relevance}` citations or an explicit `intentBasisUnavailableReason`, not both meaningful alternatives. Decision fields are `options` (each `{label, strengths, weaknesses, downstream}`), `recommendation` (`{option, rationale}` naming an option), and `maintainability`; the other kinds use their field above. Approval may also include options and a recommendation; nonempty fields belonging to another kind are rejected after optional null or empty values normalize to absent. When available, call `request_developer` with those fields directly as tool arguments, not a nested `request` wrapper. Otherwise use the Playbook checkout's `python3 scripts/request-developer.py --request '<JSON>'` with the same object. Correct validation errors before sending; include the successful rendered Markdown verbatim in the developer-facing message, not a rewritten summary. Supplied text renders literally with its line breaks preserved, never as additional Markdown structure. Neither validation nor rendering sends a question, grants approval, or resumes work.
   Rendering uses minimal Markdown backslash escapes, never numeric entities; ordinary spaces, apostrophes, punctuation, and paths remain readable in a raw terminal.
5. **Collect a blocking decision only after rendering.** For a blocking `decision`, first validate and render, then use OMP's built-in interactive question mechanism when available, carrying the same option labels/order and named recommendation. Preserve the complete rendered context in the question surface, not only in an earlier message. If the OMP session cannot collect an interactive answer, send the validated Markdown verbatim and wait for an explicit developer reply. Nonblocking requests still retain their full framing. A default, timeout selection, cancellation, or redirect is not explicit acceptance; do not resume blocked work until the developer has actually answered.

### Gate requests

Use the developer-request procedure above for document gates; [Approvals](product-documentation-process.md#approvals) owns which documents need a gate and how approval is recorded.

1. **Name the approval and what it means.** Use `kind: approval` and put the document path, revision when present, and the precise acceptance in `acceptance`. For the product vision and a PRD, ask the developer to agree that they have read the document in full and accept its content. For architecture and a system design, ask them to agree that they understand it well enough to explain it and defend its technical decisions, and agree with those decisions; do not require a full reread after each change.
2. **Brief the developer, not just the changes.** Put why approval is needed and what happens next in `context`, and keep what the developer is asked to agree to in `acceptance`, using the existing request schema rather than adding fields. A link or revision number alone is never enough.
   - **Architecture and system design:** write `context` as a briefing the developer could repeat to someone else and defend, not a list of edits. For a first approval, explain the whole design: what it does, how its main parts fit together, and each consequential decision with its reason, the alternative given up, and its cost. For a re-approval, give the same explanation for each change since the last approval, including what the document said before; name which decisions changed, including when none changed, and give purely editorial changes one line. When a reviewer objection was rejected, state it as the counterargument with the answer to it. End each point with the section that holds its detail, so the developer can read only the part they cannot yet defend.
   - **Product vision and PRD:** summarize the changes since the last approval and identify which decisions changed, including when none changed, as a guide for the full read.
   - **Leave out what does not help the developer judge the document:** review and pass counts, tracking-issue IDs, and deferred work, unless one of them is needed to defend a decision. Cite recorded evidence only as the reason for a decision, with its section.
3. **State the consequence and recording action.** Say what approval unlocks and what waits while `blocking: true`. Ask the developer to run `/playbook-hash <path>` and paste the returned entry line into `docs/user-approvals.json` once they accept the document; agents must not record it on their behalf. If the developer requests changes, follow the [document review loop](document-review.md#review-loop) and return to the same gate; make that next step explicit in the request.
4. **Finish correctly at a gate or limit.** Stopping at a developer gate, or at a limit that requires the developer, is a successful finish to the turn, not incomplete work. Render the request with the current state and the action needed to continue, then stop the blocked work rather than treating a default, timeout, cancellation, or redirect as approval.

### Friction line

When Playbook guidance, a Playbook tool, or the way you were dispatched caused avoidable work in this run — an unclear or conflicting instruction, a check that rejected valid input, missing input the dispatcher should have supplied — end your final report or message with one line per occurrence: `Friction: <what happened and what it cost>`. Omit the line when nothing qualifies, and never put it in a document the task writes. [Friction reviews](friction.md) collect these lines.

### Before you send

Check, in order: outcome first; thread named and self-contained; ordinary messages grouped within the concise limits; every anchor carries its reason; developer requests validated and rendered verbatim with full context, specific intent basis or explicit absence, and kind-specific framing; blocking decisions routed only after rendering with unchanged choices and recommendation; caveats present only if real; Friction lines present only if real.

## Evidence

These rules translate published findings on writing for interrupted, low-context, and fatigued readers. They inform the policy; they are not themselves rules to cite in output, and they are intentionally kept outside `## Rules` so compiled skills do not carry a bibliography.

- **Front-load and stay scannable.** Nielsen Norman Group, F-shaped reading pattern (<https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content-discovered/>); US federal plain-language guidance, now at Digital.gov (<https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/>) and archived at <https://github.com/GSA/plainlanguage.gov>. Supports rules 1, 5, 6. The military "bottom line up front" (BLUF) convention is the same idea.
- **Give a blatant reorientation cue; assume goal decay.** Trafton, Altmann & Brock 2005, "Huh, what was I doing? How people use environmental cues after an interruption" (<https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/154193120504900354>); Altmann & Trafton 2002, memory-for-goals model (<https://www.interruptions.net/literature/Altmann-CogSci02.pdf>); Mark, Gonzalez & Harris 2005, fragmented work (<https://ics.uci.edu/~gmark/CHI2005.pdf>). Supports rules 2, 3.
- **Attention lingers on the tasks just left.** Leroy 2009, "Why is it so hard to do my work? The challenge of attention residue when switching between work tasks," Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes (<https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2009.04.002>). Supports the reader assumptions in the `## Rules` lead and rule 3.
- **Respect working-memory and load limits.** Cowan 2001, "The magical number 4 in short-term memory" (<https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X01003922>); Sweller 1988, cognitive load during problem solving (<https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1202_4>); Lim & Dinges 2010, meta-analysis of sleep deprivation on cognition (<https://www.med.upenn.edu/uep/assets/user-content/documents/LimDinges2010MetaAnalysis.pdf>). Supports rules 4, 5.
- **Keep asks minimal and well-formed.** AHRQ Health Literacy Universal Precautions Toolkit, teach-back and "Ask Me 3" (<https://www.ahrq.gov/health-literacy/improve/precautions/tool5.html>); Raymond, "How To Ask Questions The Smart Way" (<http://www.catb.org/~esr/faqs/smart-questions.html>); Evans, "How to ask good questions" (<https://jvns.ca/blog/2016/08/31/asking-questions/>). Supports rules 8, 9.

Decision-fatigue effects are contested: large replications weakened the "willpower depletion" model (Hagger et al. 2016, <https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4971805/>) and field studies dispute a universal effect. Rule 8 therefore rests on the low cost of one skipped question, not on a strong depletion claim.
